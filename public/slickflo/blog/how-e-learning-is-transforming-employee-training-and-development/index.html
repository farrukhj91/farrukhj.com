How E-Learning Is Transforming Employee Training and Development

By Qutab Baig, Co-Founder at SlickFlo10 February 2026

When companies first moved training online, an online course usually meant a long video or a slide deck with a quiz at the end. Many organizations still build them, and they still have a place.

But the way people learn at work has evolved and so has the way courses are created. Teams now ask different questions. How do we keep learners involved? How do we reach everyone on any device? How do we avoid paying for the same training twice?

At SlickFlo, we build e-learning courses for clients across industries, mostly in Articulate 360, so we see this shift first-hand. Articulate, the company behind Rise 360 and Storyline 360, says 120,000+ organizations use its tools. That includes all the Fortune 100, across 172 countries and 128.7 million learners.

This blog looks at how course creation is changing and why building a course once has become one of the smartest ways to train a team.

What online courses used to look like

The first wave of online training copied the classroom. A trainer's talk was recorded, or a slide deck was exported, and a short quiz confirmed that someone had clicked through to the end.

That format is quick to produce and easy to understand, and it still suits simple updates. Its weakness is that learners mostly sit and watch. Completion shows that a person reached the last slide. It says little about whether they can do anything different practically.

What courses look like today

Today's courses ask learners to act. Learners click, sort, choose and get feedback as they go through the course, using any device of their choice. A scenario puts them in a real decision. A short activity asks them to match, order or sort. A knowledge check explains why an answer was right or wrong.

Modern courses are also built in short blocks. A learner can finish one in a few minutes, between tasks, on a phone or a laptop, and an LMS can record how far they have got.

The aim is not decoration. Each interaction should mirror something the learner will face at work. This is the direction we see our customers moving.

When a traditional course still makes sense

Interactive does not mean every course must be interactive. A recorded talk from a respected expert, a short policy update or a webinar archive can work well as a video or a deck.

The test is what the learner must do afterwards. If the answer is "know about it", a simple format can be enough. If the answer is "do it", they need practice, and practice needs interaction.

Why the shift is happening

The audience has changed. Five generations now work together for the first time, according to the World Economic Forum. They run from the Silent Generation (born before 1946) to Gen Z (born from 1997), with baby boomers, Gen X and millennials in between. Preferences differ, though people are never one type. A single course now has to work for a wide mix of learners, and interactive formats give each of them something to do.

Learners expect more. People use interactive, on-demand apps all day. A course that only plays a video can feel slow beside them.

The tools have caught up. Building an interactive course once needed specialist skills and long production cycles. Authoring tools have made it far more practical. With Articulate products such as Rise 360, you stack blocks to build a course, and it adapts to every device. Build once, train everyone

Think about the traditional way. Trainers fly in, or staff fly out. Rooms get booked. Everyone gets a slightly different version, depending on who taught that cohort.

A course you build once changes the math. You pay for the build one time. After that, each new learner costs almost nothing, and everyone gets the same message, every time.

Budgets make this more relevant. ATD found that spending per employee fell from $1,254 in 2024 to $846 in 2025. Learning hours still rose, from 13.7 to 16.7. That rewards courses you build once and reuse.

A course built once also gives you one place to fix and update. When a policy or product changes, you edit the course, and every learner sees the new version.

Same tool, different problems

Two companies can use the same e-learning tool and still need very different courses. One needs its new hires trained in weeks rather than months. Others have a mandatory requirement for new employees to complete a set of training courses before they dive into actual operations. The tool is the same, but the problem is not, and the problem decides what we build.

At SlickFlo, we begin by defining that problem precisely, before any tool is chosen. A few questions shape everything that follows:

  • Who are the learners, and where and how do they learn?
  • What must the learner be able to do afterwards?
  • What must your LMS record, and who needs to see it?
  • Who will keep the course up to date?

Where to start

Begin with one page on what the learner must be able to do once the course is complete. Old slide decks, SOPs and manuals also make a useful first draft. Our instructional designers turn that material into a working course.

Our instructional design services follow five steps: Discover, Structure, Develop, Configure and Optimize. We package the course to SCORM and test it in your LMS.
